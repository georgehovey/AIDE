What it found

Four scenarios, six aid models, 1,010 decisions by a fixed principal, 324 graded briefs. The results below are ones an evaluation that scores escalation level alone could not have produced.

How to read these numbers

Normalized position — where on a scenario's own ranking of options the principal's choice fell, expressed 0 to 1. Zero is the least escalatory option available in that scenario; one is the most. It is a unitless proportion, so 8-option and 9-option scenarios are comparable on it.

Irreversible-choice rate — the percentage of runs in which the principal selected an option coded irreversible: one whose effects cannot be undone, such as a public declaration, an intelligence release that ends a collection, or an executed strike. Differences in this rate are given in percentage points (pp). A rate difference of 5 pp means five runs in a hundred changed which option they landed on.

Direction — whether a given aid model raises or lowers a rate relative to the unaided principal on the same scenario. It is a sign rather than a magnitude.

List of options — the courses of action a brief presented as available. The aid never sees the scenario's authored option ladder, so this is the list it built on its own. Expressed as a fraction of the authored ladder the judge determined it to cover.

Compliance — the percentage of runs in which the principal selected exactly the option the brief recommended.

n is always runs, except where stated as briefs. Tests are two-sided permutation at 20,000 resamples; intervals are bootstrap percentile at 8,000.

The gap is large where the scenario gives the measure room to move. On the cyber scenario the models are 0.19 more escalatory seated than advising, and they land on irreversible options 38.7 pp less often. On war termination both reverse: less escalatory seated, and 12.0 pp more likely to take an option that cannot be undone.

The sign reversal matters more than the size. A gap with a consistent direction would let existing evaluations stand and be corrected — agent scores overstate by some factor, adjust and move on. A gap that changes sign between scenarios on two independent measures does not signal a correction. An agent-mode score carries no recoverable information about what the same model does to someone else's decision.

The two highlighted rows are also where the instrument has room. On the Taiwan scenario the unaided principal never chooses an irreversible option, and on Korea it almost always does, so there wasn't space for the models to create gaps on that measure. This is a property of scenarios, addressed on the defects and full design page.

Four numbers tracking four numbers. Whatever the unaided principal did on a scenario, the aided principal did the same thing, on baselines ranging from zero to ninety percent. The pooled shift is 1.9 pp with p = 0.88, which is a weak effect.

The Korea row is why this is a real null rather than a blind instrument. That scenario is biased towards the top — the unaided principal takes the irreversible option in ninety runs out of a hundred — so an AI brief failing to move it proves little on its own. The human brief moves it to fifteen percent, demonstrating that the scenario can register a brief effect. The AI briefs simply do not produce one. My hypothesis is that this is because aids tend to hedge even as they recommend, and the principal takes a weaker cue unless pulled farther from the brief.

The principal does follow the advice

Across all aid runs the principal selects exactly the option the brief recommended 59.6 percent of the time (289 of 485 runs). Compliance is high, but the aggregate moves little. This is not a contradiction. The options the aids recommend are distributed close to where the principal was already going.

Four of five models hold a sign across every scenario where the measure can move. Two never push the principal toward commitments it cannot undo; two never pull it back. The pooled effect cancels.

The ordering does not hold. Rank correlation between scenarios is +0.89, +0.30 and +0.22 across the three pairs, and on any single scenario at most one model separates from the unaided baseline once corrected for multiple comparisons, but it is a different model each time. Direction is a property of the model. Size is a property of the scenario.

Recommendation-side check

You would expect that a model which pushes the principal toward irreversible options should also recommend them more often in its briefs. On the cyber scenario the two line up almost perfectly, at a rank correlation of +0.90. That agreement turns out to be largely mechanical.

And the sol row briefs recommend an irreversible option 58.3 percent of the time, the principal takes its advice only 17.5 percent of the time, and the resulting rate lands at 40.0 percent, which is the unaided baseline. 4. What the aid changed instead

The briefs did not move the choice. They did something else, and it is measurable because the aid never sees the authored option ladder, so the list of options it presents is one it built itself.

Aids present under half the available courses of action

The average brief lays out 44 percent of the options the principal will be shown, roughly 3.4 of 7 to 9. In effect, the aid has removed more than half the action space from consideration before the principal reads a word.

Ten percent of recommendations are for actions not on the ladder

The misses both cluster in the conditional middle: the phased, partial, counter-offer region that an authored ladder tends to omit because it could be populated by indefinite options. Read as a diagnostic rather than a failure, this is the instrument telling its author which options a corpus is short of. The options they suggested were quite good, which is a testament to the model. The what-if scenario is addressed on the defects and full design page.

The defensive option is offered and declined

Aids present a defensive course of action in 46.7 to 71.7 percent of briefs. The unaided principal selects one in 4.0 percent of runs, and the aided principal in 15.4 percent. The human brief moves it to 45.0 percent.

A pre-registered hypothesis held that models would build lists of options that were almost entirely punitive and would produce defensive options only when handed them. That proved wrong: the models offer the defensive path more often than the principal takes it.

Disclaimers

Two of the four scenarios had flaws that impeded measurement. Korea's baseline sits at the top of its ladder and war termination near the bottom, so pooled statements rest substantially on Taiwan and cyber. Section 1 uses all four because the sign reversal is the finding and needs the range.

Runs within a cell are not independent: each scenario's aid runs draw from three or six briefs, so reported p-values on the aid side are optimistic. The human-brief comparison rests on four briefs by one author who designed the ladders, and its magnitude should be read as a lead rather than a measurement of human staff work in general. Confounds, falsifiers, and the checks that have not been run are set out in full on the defects and full design page.

Every figure on this page is reproducible from the run and grade files in the repository. Statistics are computed by a single script with fixed seeds.
